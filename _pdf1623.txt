Ubiquitous data-driven framework for traffic 
emission estimation and policy evaluation
 
Songhua Hu

Quantifying urban traffic emission dynamics is critical for evaluating 
decarbonization policies, yet existing models often lack resolution, timeliness 
or scalability. Ubiquitous urban data offer new opportunities for large-scale, 
fine-grained and near-real-time emission estimation. Here we present a 
data-driven framework that integrates traffic camera footage with mobile 
phone data to estimate citywide vehicular emissions. Applied to Manhattan, 
New York, our method captures substantial spatiotemporal variation in 
emissions across hours, days and road segments. Omitting fine-grained inputs, 
such as traffic signals, speed variation or fleet heterogeneity, introduces 
average uncertainties of −49% to +25% in emission estimates. We further 
evaluate the early impacts of Manhattan’s congestion pricing programme, 
finding that 8 weeks after the implementation, traffic volumes declined by 
10%, resulting in a 16–22% drop in emissions. Our approach enables timely, 
high-resolution policy assessment using widely available urban big data, 
offering a practical and transferable tool for supporting climate action.
Transport contributes nearly 25% of global energy-related carbon 
dioxide (CO2) emissions and stands as a critical barrier to achieving 
net-zero climate targets1–3. Road transport is the largest contributor 
to these emissions. According to the US Environmental Protection 
Agency (EPA), transport accounted for 28% (the largest) of total CO2 
emissions in 2022, with road transport accounting for 80% (ref. 4) of 
total transport emissions. Similarly, in the European Union, the Euro-
pean Environment Agency reports that transport contributes 25% of 
total CO2 emissions, with road transport making up 75% (ref. 5). Beyond 
CO2 emissions, transport is also a major source of air pollutants, par-
ticularly nitrogen oxides (NOx), particulate matter (PM) and carbon 
monoxide (CO)4–6, resulting in increasingly severe climate change, 
health risks and ecological deterioration5–8.
Despite advances in green fuels, low-carbon technologies and 
sustainability policies, road transport emissions have continued to rise 
globally over decades and are projected to increase further in the com-
ing years2. As cities not only account for the largest share of road traffic 
emissions but also have the highest population densities and exposure 
to traffic-related air pollution and its associated health impacts3,9, 
there is an urgent need for developing a scalable framework capable 
of accurately and promptly estimating emissions at urban scales, both 
under baseline conditions and in response to policy interventions2,10. 
For instance, on 5 January 2025, the United States launched its first 
congestion pricing programme in Manhattan11, aimed at reducing 
traffic congestion and emissions. In Europe, similar policies, such as 
London’s Low Emission Zone and Milan’s Area C, have been in place for 
years. However, the extent of their actual impact on emissions remains 
unclear12. Moreover, given the inherent spatiotemporal variability of 
traffic patterns, traffic emissions fluctuate substantially across space 
and time13–15. Effective traffic emission models must therefore not 
only estimate total emissions accurately but also resolve their spati-
otemporal distributions, which are essential for targeted mitigation, 
aetiological analysis and advancing environmental justice.
Current methods for estimating citywide traffic emission invento-
ries typically rely on macroscopic models, which use static approaches 
to estimate long-term, macro-scale averages16. However, their coarse

with origin–destination (OD) flows extracted from anonymized and 
aggregated mobile phone location data (MPLD) to reconstruct citywide 
traffic dynamics. These are then combined with microscopic emis-

including traffic signals, speed variation and fleet heterogeneity; and 
(3) evaluate the impacts of various decarbonization strategies on emis-
sion reduction, including congestion pricing, travel mode shifts and 
departure time shifts. The approach is highly scalable, as it bypasses 
the need for intrusive, high-frequency vehicle tracking and instead 
relies on globally ubiquitous data sources. The approach can also 
generate near-real-time outcomes as all inputs are updated either in 
real time (live traffic footage) or daily (MPLD). This enables large-scale, 
near-real-time implementation and provides a unified platform for 
rapid and dynamic emission estimation and policy evaluation to inform 
emission mitigation strategies.
Results
Fleet composition and signal control
We show that fleet composition can be extracted from low-resolution 
camera footage using computer vision methods based on vehicle 
appearance, without relying on license plate recognition (see ‘Vehicle 
spatial resolution and low update frequency (typically every 1–5 years) 
limit their ability to capture near-real-time dynamics and fine-scale 
spatiotemporal variability10,17–20. To address these, microscopic mod-
els have been developed, enabling much finer-resolution estimates 
tailored to specific application needs. While these models offer higher 
resolution and dynamics, they require granular inputs that are difficult 
to obtain and computationally expensive to process12,21. For example, 
for project-level estimates, the Motor Vehicle Emission Simulator 
(MOVES)22, the regulatory standard in the United States, requires 
1-Hz speed profiles for every on-road vehicle. Although advances in 
intelligent transportation systems have enabled large-scale traffic 
data collection, such as high-frequency trajectory data from probe 
vehicles3,12,23, multimodal traffic counts from city cameras24 and vehicle 
registration information with detailed vehicle configurations15,21,25, 
accessing such data remains limited owing to privacy concerns sur-
rounding individual locations and identities. This hinders the scal-
ability of bottom-up models and always leads to the omission of key 
emission-related factors in practical implementations26. For instance, 
traffic signal is widely acknowledged as a key determinant of vehicular 
emissions in urban settings as it substantially changes stop-and-go 
behaviour27,28, yet it is rarely incorporated into citywide emission inven-
tories owing to a lack of accessible data.

achieves 92.9% average accuracy (range: 81–100%) in identifying 12 
vehicle types defined by MOVES22. Applied to 331 cameras in Manhattan, 
the model reveals distinct hourly volume profiles for different vehicle 
types (Fig. 1a): passenger cars and buses follow a bimodal weekday 
distribution, trucks and motorcycles follow a unimodal pattern and 
school buses show sharp dual peaks aligned with school schedules. 
Spatial heterogeneity is also evident, with buses concentrated in 
midtown and trucks more frequently observed in peripheral areas 
(Supplementary Fig. 2). For validation, we compare camera-derived 
traffic volumes with nearby automated traffic recorders (ATRs) and 
find high consistency (correlation: 0.93) (Fig. 1b). We further compare 
vehicle type distributions derived from our model with the manually 
labelled ground truth and a baseline model (You Only Look Once (YOLO) 
v8 (ref. 29)). While the baseline underestimates the share of passen-
ger cars by 15% and overestimates trucks and buses by 7%, our model 
reduces these biases to within ±5% (Fig. 1c).
We show that traffic signal, another important factor in urban 
traffic emission estimation27,28, can also be identified from traffic cam-
era footage. As illustrated in Fig. 1d, clear vehicle arrival and queuing 
patterns during red and green phases at a signalized intersection can 
be observed and discretized into detailed signal phase information 
(mean absolute error: 2–5 s) (see ‘Traffic signal timing inference’ 
in Methods). The inferred signal timing is then used to reconstruct 
second-by-second driving cycles from average speed profiles: vehicle 
speed is set to zero during red phases, while green phases are modelled 
with a typical accelerate–cruise–decelerate pattern (Fig. 1e). These 
adjustments notably impact the distribution of vehicle operating 
modes (OpModes) used by MOVES to estimate emission rates. As shown 
in Fig. 1f, incorporating signal control substantially increases idle 
statuses (OpMode 1), particularly on residential roads owing to longer 
red phases. In addition, there is a marked shift from low-speed, gentle 
driving (OpMode 12) to high-speed, aggressive driving (OpModes 
22–38), indicating more frequent acceleration and deceleration events 
induced by signal controls.
Traffic network extrapolation
Cameras cover only part of the road network, yet citywide emission 
estimation requires network-wide traffic information. We show that 
integrating camera observations with MPLD-derived OD matrices can 
produce reliable full-network traffic flow estimations (see ‘Traffic net-
work extrapolation’ in Methods); at no point were datasets merged at 
the individual level, nor were attempts made to reidentify anonymized 
mobility data. MPLD offers broad coverage and near-real-time 
updates on human travel, but as a sampled dataset, it suffers from 
incomplete population representation30. To correct for this, we cali-
brate traffic analysis zone-level sampling rates using an integrated 
optimization-dynamic traffic assignment (DTA) approach31, improving 
the correlation between assigned volumes and camera-derived vol-
umes from 0.34 to 0.94 (Fig. 2a). The assigned volumes are then used 
to estimate speeds via a road-specific fundamental diagram (FD)32, 
which characterizes the relationship among traffic volume, speed 
and density. As shown in Fig. 2b, a clear negative relationship between 
traffic density and speed is well captured, with the degree of curvature 
varying by road type. After calibration by real-world observations, the 
speed estimated from the FD can achieve a correlation of 0.87 with 
ground truth (Supplementary Fig. 3). This calibrated FD serves as a 
foundation for assessing policy impacts, for example, modelling how 
shifts in travel demand propagate through network traffic dynamics 
to ultimately affect emissions (that is, travel demand → traffic volume 
→ traffic speed → traffic emission).
Figure 2c,d shows the daily profiles of estimated traffic speed and 
volume, aggregated at 5-min intervals, by road type. Clear diurnal pat-
terns are observed, with distinct morning and evening peaks character-
ized by increased volumes and reduced speeds (11–21 miles per hour 
(mph)). Distinct traffic flow patterns also emerge across road types: 
motorways show the highest volumes and speeds, whereas residential 
roads show the lowest. Figure 2e,f presents the spatial distributions 
of estimated traffic speed and volume, revealing fine-grained spatial 
heterogeneity that would be obscured under conventional zonal aver-
age spatial allocation methods14,19,33: traffic speeds are notably lower 
in midtown and downtown Manhattan, areas known for high conges-
tion due to dense intersections and heavy travel demand, while traffic 
volumes are notably higher along major corridors and near entry and 
exit points (tunnels and bridges).
Spatiotemporal emission mapping
By inputting road-level traffic details into MOVES (see ‘On-road vehic-
ular emission estimation’ in Methods), we estimate annual on-road 
vehicular emissions in Manhattan for 2020 to be 11.67 ktCO, 2.30 MtCO2, 
2.05 kt of NOx and 0.12 kt of PM2.5. Compared with the 2020 on-road 
National Emissions Inventory of the US EPA34, our estimates are higher 
across all pollutants: 22.52% for CO, 36.35% for CO2, 36.77% for NOx and 
48.46% for PM2.5. These discrepancies remain substantial even when 
accounting for the total uncertainty of 4.51–6.75% in the yearly inven-
tory caused by input estimate errors. Consistent with previous studies 
reporting that emission inventories in US cities are under-reported 
by 18.3% on average10, we attribute this discrepancy to the absence of 
high-resolution traffic data in EPA models and to differences in how 
road traffic changes during the COVID-19 pandemic were captured13,35,36.
Figure 3a–d presents the spatial distribution of road-level emission 
densities. A subset of road segments with available air quality sensor 
data was used to validate the estimated emission concentrations, yield-
ing a correlation of 0.87–0.91 between observed and modelled values 
(Supplementary Fig. 6). Spatial patterns are similar across pollutants, 
with concentrations notably higher near major entry and exit points 
connecting Manhattan to neighbouring boroughs. Emission densities 
on these bridges and tunnels are 318.90% (CO) to 513.38% (NOx) higher 
than the network average, and rise further during peak hours. Emission 
densities also vary substantially by road type: motorways, comprising 
only 16.40% of the network, contribute 30.74% of total CO2 emissions. 
Even within the same road type, emission densities show wide varia-
tion. Relative to road-type-specific averages, CO2 density ranges from 
−100.00% to +391.08% on residential roads and −96.37% to +156.12% 
on primary roads. Moreover, we notice that pollutants such as CO and 
NOx are more concentrated in city centres, probably because slower, 
stop-and-go traffic in these areas leads to incomplete combustion and 
frequent acceleration events that elevate emissions. These results 
highlight that relying on road-type averages for citywide extrapolation 
can obscure substantial spatial heterogeneity and introduce systematic 
biases in emission estimates.
Temporal variations in emissions are also notable. CO2 emissions 
show hourly fluctuations from −86.45% (at 3:00 a.m.) to +77.23% (at 
4:00 p.m.) relative to the daily average, with peak hours (7:00–9:00 
a.m. and 4:00–6:00 p.m.) contributing 38.92% of total daily emissions 
(Fig. 3e). Day-to-day variations are also notable owing to both regular 
weekly cycles and irregular disruptions (Fig. 3f). By analysing 2 years 
of MPLD, we find that in 2021, compared with the daily average, CO2 
emissions dropped by 90.02% during the February snowstorm and 
by 61.78% during Hurricane Henri, but increased by 36.80% on Black 
Friday. Other notable events are also well captured, including reduc-
tions during the December 2020 snowstorm and the May 2021 thun-
derstorm, as well as an increase around Christmas. In 2020, emissions 
averaged 32.69% lower than in 2021 owing to the COVID-19 pandemic, 
with the largest daily drop of 87.21% occurring on March 22 during the 
national lockdown37.

in Fig. 4a, omitting traffic signals underestimates total emissions by 
6.5% for NOx, 15.17% for CO and 48.64% for PM2.5, while its impact on CO2

controls on vehicle acceleration patterns in urban settings (Fig. 1f), 
which result in higher emission rates of NOx, CO and PM2.5 compared 
with steady cruising. The discrepancy is especially pronounced on 
residential roads, where emissions are underestimated by 11.16% for 
CO2, 29.61% for NOx, 43.10% for CO and 66.85% for PM2.5. This is prob-
ably due to the high density of traffic lights and low traffic priority on 
residential roads, which results in more frequent stop-and-go driving 
that substantially increases emissions.
Figure 4b shows that replacing road-level fleet composition with 
citywide averages leads to overestimations of NOx by 24.79% and PM2.5 by 
23.16%, with minimal impacts on CO and CO2. This is probably because 
citywide averages reflect a broader spatial distribution of heavy-duty 
vehicles, which are primary contributors to NOx and PM2.5. This also 
explains the large road-level variation observed in these pollutants when 
average fleet composition is applied. Figure 4c shows that substituting 
road-specific speeds with road-type-specific averages underestimates 
total emissions by 10.99% for PM2.5, 12.00% for CO, 13.13% for CO2 and 
14.55% for NOx. The effect is particularly pronounced on higher-class 
roads, which is plausible as they show the highest original speed lev-
els and variation, but are obscured when using aggregated averages 
(Fig. 2c). Finally, Fig. 4d shows that removing all camera-derived infor-
mation—including fleet composition (replaced by citywide averages), 
signal control (omitted) and calibrated volumes (replaced with uncal-
ibrated volumes)—leads to underestimations of total emissions by 
31.96% for CO2, 34.72% for NOx, 35.03% for CO and 44.72% for PM2.5. These 
results underscore the importance of incorporating camera footage to 
enhance the accuracy of urban emission inventories.
Policy evaluation
We show that the proposed framework can effectively quantify the 
effects of various transportation decarbonization policies, such as 
travel mode shifts, departure time shifts and congestion pricing38. While 
the first two policy scenarios are based on simulated input changes, the 
congestion pricing case leverages real-world observational data (see 
‘On-road vehicular emission estimation’ in Methods). Figure 5a shows 
that shifting 10% of departures out of peak hours leads to emission 
reductions of 3.64% for CO, 5.89% for PM2.5, 6.57% for NOx and 5.96% 
for CO2. These reductions become more pronounced with a 30% shift, 
reaching 9.38%, 14.46%, 16.57% and 14.95% for CO, PM2.5, NOx and CO2, 
respectively. Note that as total travel demand remains unchanged, 
the observed decreases are mainly due to the alleviation of peak-hour 
congestion. Similarly, Fig. 5b shows that encouraging a shift from driv-
ing to public transit substantially reduces emissions, with additional 
benefits from reduced congestion. A 10% shift lowers CO by 16.16%, CO2 
by 17.31%, NOx by 13.90% and PM2.5 by 15.43%; at 30%, the reductions rise 
to 40.54%, 41.24%, 34.54% and 37.84%, respectively.
Figure 5c shows the growing impact of congestion pricing over 
time. Emissions decrease by 3.56% for CO, 9.57% for CO2, 12.18% for 
NOx and 13.69% for PM2.5 2 weeks after implementation; after 8 weeks, 
the reductions reach 16.16%, 18.07%, 21.91% and 22.72%, respectively. 
These reductions result from combined declines in traffic volume, 
heavy-duty vehicle share and congestion, including a 10.34% decrease 
in passenger cars, a 13.76% reduction in trucks and a 12.18% increase in 
average traffic speed by week 8 (Extended Data Fig. 1). These changes 
are timely captured by our framework and align well with independ-
ent traffic observations from the E-ZPass system39 and Google Maps40 
(Supplementary Section 1.7). Although an overall decrease is observed, 
these reductions are not uniformly distributed. As shown in Fig. 5d–g, 
higher-class road segments show greater reductions compared with

Discussion
As cities worldwide pursue ambitious decarbonization targets, accurate 
quantification and timely evaluation of emerging decarbonization 
policies are critical. In this study, we present the first unified, scalable 
framework that integrates ubiquitous, multi-source data to generate 
high-resolution, near-real-time vehicular emission inventories at the 
city scale. Methodologically, we introduce several innovations: (1) a 
vehicle type classifier that extracts fleet composition from camera 
footage in full alignment with the official emission model, (2) a citywide 
signal inference module that reconstructs second-by-second driving 
cycles directly from camera footage and (3) an optimization-assignment 
model that fuses sparse camera observations with MPLD-derived OD 
flows to extrapolate network-wide traffic dynamics. Together, these 
advances bridge the gap between macroscopic inventory models that 
lack spatiotemporal dynamics and microscopic models that are often 
unscalable. In addition, with fully live data streams, our approach is 
capable of producing near-real-time emission estimates, offering 
great potential for rapid policy evaluation and adaptive management. 
We show this through an empirical evaluation of the newly imple-
mented Manhattan congestion pricing policy, along with analyses 
of a wide range of real-world events and simulated travel demand 
management strategies.
Our findings challenge the adequacy of conventional traffic emis-
sion modelling practices41,42. Most existing inventories rely on average 
traffic and static fleet assumptions, potentially misrepresenting where 
and when emissions are actually concentrated26. In Manhattan, we 
observe substantial spatiotemporal variations in emissions, which can 
distort policy impact assessments and misdirect mitigation resources. 
For instance, while citywide emissions targets may appear to be met, 
specific neighbourhoods may still experience excessive pollution lev-
els, undermining environmental goals and exacerbating environmental 
injustice8. By contrast, high-resolution emissions mapping enables 
more targeted and effective policy interventions. For example, we find 
that emission densities near major connectors are 319–513% higher 
than the average, and emissions also surge during peak hours and 
specific events. These spatiotemporal hotspots suggest opportunities 
for hyperlocal interventions, such as dynamic signal optimization or 
peak-hour delivery restrictions, to more effectively reduce emissions 
at critical times and locations.
Another central finding of this work is the quantifiable biases 
introduced by simplifying assumptions common in emission invento-
ries. Omitting traffic signals underestimates PM2.5 by 49%. Given that 
over 330,000 traffic signals operate in the United States, neglecting 
their impact may introduce substantial errors in emission inventories.

estimates17. One key reason is that emission models are composed of 
nonlinear, nonadditive functions, making them highly sensitive to 
input resolution26. These structural limitations raise concerns about 
the reliability of self-reported inventories. Previous research has docu-
mented discrepancies between traditional and data-driven estimates 
ranging from −145% to +63% (refs. 10,13). Our results contribute to this 
evidence and underscore the need for high-resolution inputs.
Beyond methodological improvements, our framework enables 
causal evaluation of policy interventions by explicitly linking travel 
demand, traffic flow and emissions. While current decarbonization 
efforts largely focus on technological solutions (for example, electrifi-
cation and renewable energy), we find that travel demand management 
strategies are also highly effective in advancing net-zero goals1,43. For 
example, shifting 10% of vehicle departures out of peak hours cuts emis-
sions by 4–7%, but an even more effective policy would be redirecting 
these trips to public transit, which would lower emissions by 14–17% 
(ref. 44). These nonlinear gains from driving trip reduction and conges-
tion alleviation highlight the importance of prioritizing demand man-
agement strategies in accelerating the decarbonization transition2,45. 
The real-world rollout of congestion pricing in Manhattan serves as 
a compelling validation case: within 8 weeks, emissions declined by 
16–22% across pollutants, driven by a 10–14% drop in driving trips and 
a 12% increase in traffic speed. Importantly, these reductions were 
not uniform but varied substantially by road type and location. Roads 
outside the congestion relief zone experience on average 5–9% less 
reduction in emissions, and some segments even saw increases due 
to traffic detours or demand shifts13. These disparities highlight the 
need for real-time, high-resolution monitoring to identify communi-
ties that may be underserved by such policies and to enable adaptive 
refinements that ensure both effectiveness and equity.
While our case study focuses on Manhattan, the proposed frame-
work is broadly transferable. Public traffic cameras are deployed in 
numerous cities globally, and the rapid development of computer 
vision technologies has made large-scale, camera-based traffic moni-
toring increasingly feasible24. MPLD are also increasingly available 
across the world through commercial and academic partnerships, 
showing great potential across domains such as transportation 
planning, environmental monitoring and public health37,46. Nota-
bly, our method does not rely on fine-grained vehicle trajectories, 
high-resolution cameras or vehicle registration records. Instead, it 
uses sparse camera feeds and aggregated OD flows, making it transfer-
able even to cities with limited data infrastructures47. Nevertheless, we 
acknowledge that practical barriers remain. Access to traffic camera 
data is not always feasible across jurisdictions owing to differences in 
privacy regulations and data-sharing policies, and obtaining large-scale 
MPLD from commercial providers can be costly. These factors may 
limit the immediate scalability of the framework, but also highlight the 
need to improve data accessibility for public benefit. By showing the 
scientific value of these datasets and quantifying the substantial biases 
introduced to emission estimates when they are omitted, this study 
calls for greater investment in open data systems and cross-agency col-
laboration to support more accurate and adaptive climate governance.
Several limitations remain. First, our emission estimates focus 
solely on on-road running emissions and exclude upstream emis-
sions from vehicle manufacturing. Future work should incorporate

Methods
Our method integrates multi-source ubiquitous data to prepare 
near-real-time, high-resolution inputs for emission estimation (see 
full framework in Extended Data Fig. 2). Specifically, traffic camera 
footage is used to extract fleet composition, traffic volume and signal 
timing at selected intersections. These localized observations are 
then combined with mobile phone data to extrapolate traffic pat-
terns across the entire road network. The resulting road-level fleet 
composition and traffic flow patterns are input into the MOVES to 
produce a citywide on-road vehicular emission inventory. We select 
Manhattan as our case study owing to its urgent decarbonization 
goals38, the recent implementation of congestion pricing11 and rich 
public data sources.

Vehicle information extraction
A total of 331 traffic cameras are installed in Manhattan (Supplementary 
Fig. 1), each providing live public feeds consisting of still images 
updated every 2–5 s. This study starts by scraping live images from 
these cameras and concatenating them into hourly video streams. 
The videos are then processed using computer vision techniques to 
detect, track, classify and count vehicles by type. The resulting vehicle 
data are mapped to specific road segments based on spatial proximity, 
directional alignment and scene similarity (Supplementary Section 1.1).
We use the YOLOv8 network29 to detect on-road vehicles in each 
video frame. YOLOv8 is a state-of-the-art one-stage object detection 
model that offers superior accuracy and computational efficiency 
compared with previous architectures29. Following detection, each 
vehicle is assigned a bounding box indicating its location within the 
frame. The coordinates of these bounding boxes are then recorded for 
subsequent vehicle tracking and type classification.
Given the prevalence of stationary vehicles on urban roads, it is 
crucial to identify these still vehicles to prevent double-counting the 
same vehicle across frames. A common solution is object tracking50,51, 
which links the same object across consecutive frames based on visual 
similarity. However, most tracking algorithms require high frame 
rates and high-resolution inputs—conditions not met in this study. To 
address this challenge, we design a custom tracking algorithm that 
relies solely on positional closeness to identify and match still objects 
across consecutive frames:

To classify vehicle type, the default YOLOv8 model includes only 
5 vehicle types (car, motorcycle, bus, bike and truck), which does not 
align with the 13 source types used in MOVES. To address this, we con-
struct a labelled dataset using the Bing Image API to retrieve images 
corresponding to each MOVES-defined vehicle type (Supplementary 
Section 1.2). This process yields a dataset of 6,382 images covering 
12 vehicle types: combination truck, electric car, intercity bus, light 
commercial truck, motorcycle, motorhome, passenger car, passenger 
truck, refuse truck, single-unit truck, school bus and transit bus. Due 
to the difficulty in visually distinguishing between short-haul and 
long-haul vehicles, we merge them into a single type. Also, as vehicle age 
cannot be reliably inferred from images alone, we apply the citywide 
vehicle age distribution by source type based on New York City (NYC) 
vehicle registration statistics.
On the basis of this dataset, we evaluate 10 state-of-the-art image 
classifiers and select the best model based on both performance and 
efficiency. The model training process and performance metrics are 
summarized in Extended Data Fig. 3. Among all models, ConvNeXt52 
achieves the best performance on our datasets. We therefore apply it 
to vehicles detected by YOLOv8 to assign each vehicle a more detailed 
type. To further evaluate model performance on real-world traffic 
camera footage, we randomly selected 20 vehicle images from each 
camera footage and manually labelled their vehicle types. These 
ground truths were then used to assess the performance of different 
classifiers (Fig. 1f).
Finally, road-level, vehicle-type-specific traffic volume is esti-
mated by counting the number of vehicle bounding boxes that cross 
virtual detectors, which are manually placed perpendicular to the traf-
fic flow direction on each road segment. For cameras covering multiple 
roads or directions, separate sets of detectors are assigned accord-
ingly (Supplementary Fig. 1). To avoid double-counting, stationary 
vehicle identification is applied to detect and remove vehicles appear-
ing repeatedly across frames. The extracted traffic volumes are vali-
dated by comparing hourly camera-derived counts with volume data 
from nearby ATRs. Validation results are detailed in Supplementary 
Section 1.3.
Traffic signal timing inference
Traffic signal timing can be extracted from traffic camera footage based 
on vehicle arrival time series. Specifically, we convert the arrival time 
series into a binary signal, assigning a value of 1 to time steps with more 
than one vehicle arrival and 0 otherwise. We then identify intervals of 
consecutive 1 s and 0 s to estimate green and red phases:
Red time = mode ({t0|t0 ≥10 s}), green time = mode ({t1|t1 ≥10 s})
(2)
where t0 is the duration of continuous 0 s (no vehicle arrivals) and t1 
is the duration of continuous 1 s (arrivals). Intervals shorter than 10 s 
are excluded.
To avoid overestimating red time during night-time, when sparse 
arrivals may lead to no arrivals even during green phases owing to low 
traffic volume, we restrict the analysis to daytime traffic from 8:00 a.m. 
to 6:00 p.m. In addition, to ensure compatibility with vehicular emis-
sion models, we simplify complex signal timing schemes. For example, 
exclusive left-turn phases are merged with straight-through phases 
from the same entrance, and transitional phases such as yellow and 
all-red intervals are omitted, focusing solely on effective red and 
green phases.
Manhattan contains 2,862 signalized intersections, of which 309 
are covered by cameras. Signal timings for these intersections are 
inferred directly, while the remaining intersections are assigned aver-
age signal timing values based on intersection type within their cor-
responding Thiessen polygons. We validate the model by comparing 
inferred timings with manual observations at 60 randomly selected 
intersections (10 per type), as summarized in Supplementary Table 1.
Traffic network extrapolation
To extrapolate limited camera observations to the entire network, traf-
fic assignment is typically used30,53. In this study, we first use MPLD to 
construct an initial OD matrix. Next, camera-derived traffic volumes are 
used as ground truth to calibrate the traffic assignment model. Finally, 
traffic speeds are estimated using the FD based on the assigned traffic 
volumes and corresponding traffic densities32.
We use MPLD provided by Cuebiq54 to derive the initial OD matrix. 
The dataset comprises anonymized location records from over 
1.75 million anonymized mobile devices within NYC during the study 
time frame, representing approximately 21.49% of the population of 
NYC. We extract all trips intersecting Manhattan and identify driving 
trips based on the following criteria55: trip distances between 1 mi and 
500 mi, durations >5 min and maximum GPS speed >5 mph. Selected 
trips are then aggregated at the traffic analysis zone level to construct 
the OD matrix. As MPLD captures only a subset of total travel, we weight 
the MPLD-based OD matrix using census-block-group-level penetration 
rates, calculated as the ratio of observed devices to the total population 
within each region (Supplementary Section 1.5).
We use hourly aggregated MPLD from 2020 and 2021 to ana-
lyse hourly and daily fluctuations, and from the first 8 weeks of 
2024 and 2025 to assess the impact of congestion pricing. To cap-
ture passing-through trips, we include road networks within and 
surrounding Manhattan. Traffic signal timing inferred from camera 
footage is integrated into the road cost function when calculating 
path travel time. The complete assignment network and the network 
configurations, along with simulated traffic volumes, are illustrated 
in Extended Data Fig. 4.
We use DTALite31,56,57, an open-source mesoscopic traffic simulator, 
to conduct DTA. DTALite integrates Newell’s simplified kinematic wave

On-road vehicular emission estimation
The MOVES of the US EPA is the regulatory model for estimating 
emissions from mobile sources22. This study uses the latest version, 
MOVES4. MOVES estimates emissions based on instantaneous energy 
demand, which is determined by a combination of power requirements, 
vehicle configurations and operating modes. To approximate engine 
load, MOVES uses surrogate metrics: vehicle-specific power (VSP) for 
light-duty vehicles and scaled tractive power (STP) for heavy-duty 
vehicles. The second-by-second VSP and STP are calculated as follows61:

drag, respectively; m is vehicle mass (t); M in VSP represents the fixed 
mass factor (t); and M in STP serves as a scaling factor to align STP 
ranges with those of VSP.
Based on VSP (or STP), MOVES classifies driving activity into dis-
crete OpModes, each associated with a specific emission rate (g s−1). 
These OpModes capture variations in engine load and driving behav-
iour; higher speeds and accelerations result in elevated VSP (or STP) 
values, which correspond to increased fuel consumption and higher 
emissions. An example of how emission rates vary by OpMode across 
different source types is shown in Supplementary Fig. 4.
Running MOVES is computationally intensive, making it impracti-
cal for large-scale transportation networks with dynamic changes62,63. 
To enhance modelling efficiency, this study uses MOVES-Matrix, which 
is a pre-processed version of MOVES that runs the model iteratively 
to generate a comprehensive matrix of emission rates covering all 
possible input combinations. This approach is 200 times faster while 
generating results identical to those obtained from running MOVES 
directly62,63. In this study, we focus on preparing high-resolution, 
road-level data for MOVES-Matrix, including fleet composition, driv-
ing cycles and road attributes, while the source-type-specific age dis-
tribution and meteorological conditions are represented as citywide 
averages. Specifically, meteorological inputs are derived from his-
torical records across all weather stations in NYC, while the vehicle age 
distribution is based on NYC vehicle registration statistics by source 
type (Supplementary Fig. 5).
Note that this study focuses exclusively on on-road running emis-
sions; emissions from vehicle starts, hoteling, evaporation and other 
off-network sources are not included. In addition, this study focuses 
on improving the accuracy and resolution of emission estimates by 
enhancing vehicle activity inputs. The accuracy of emission factors 
themselves, as defined within MOVES, is beyond the scope of this study.

We examine three key transport decarbonization policies: travel mode 
shift, departure time shift and congestion pricing. The first three are 
simulated scenarios, and the corresponding inputs are modified 
as follows:

(1)	 Travel mode shift: The driving demand is reduced by 10%, 20%

comparison. All public holidays and special events in NYC were 
excluded from the analysis to ensure that observed changes 
in traffic primarily reflect the effects of congestion pricing. 
Although the congestion relief zone includes only roads at and 
below 60th Street, we evaluate the policy’s impact on emissions

in vehicle speeds throughout the congestion relief zone. Valida-

Validation and uncertainty analysis
Citywide emission inventory is inherently difficult to validate owing to 
the absence of ground-truth data. Therefore, we focus on validating all 
key inputs, including traffic volume, fleet composition, signal timing 
and traffic speeds. As each model-inferred input introduces uncer-
tainty, we conduct a comprehensive uncertainty analysis by comparing 
emissions estimated from model-inferred inputs with those estimated 
from ground truth. Both the total uncertainty, obtained by replacing 
all inputs with ground truth simultaneously, and the component-wise 
uncertainty, evaluated by varying one input at a time while keeping 
others fixed, are quantified. These uncertainties are assessed at two 
spatiotemporal resolutions: (1) yearly, citywide totals, which align 
with how official inventories are typically reported, and (2) hourly, 
road-segment-level estimates, which reflect the uncertainty intro-
duced to fine-grained estimates. The detailed results are provided in 
Supplementary Section 1.8.
To further validate our results, we compare a subset of our emis-
sion estimates with independent measurements collected by City 
Scanner64, a mobile sensing platform mounted on vehicles to monitor 
on-road air pollutants such as PM and NOx. In total, five vehicles oper-
ated in NYC in January–February 2020 and September–December 2021 
to collect PM and NOx concentration data. These measurements are 
aggregated to the hourly, road-segment level and compared with our 
modelled PM2.5 and NOx concentrations estimated by the air quality 
dispersion model AERMOD65. The detailed outcomes are reported in 
Supplementary Section 1.6.
