📉 ff.1.2 From 51 billion to 0

Substack ↗

In a nutshell, Bill Gates says there are two numbers to take to heart about climate change. The first is 51 billion: the tonnes of CO2-equivalent we typically emit every year, give or take some ups and downs from year to year. That's where we are today. The second is zero: that's where we need to get to. (Bill Gates, How to Avoid a Climate Disaster)

🛴 ff.1.3 Will e-scooters save us?

Substack ↗

80% of trips in the United States are shorter than 16 km.

The distribution of human trips follows, needless to say, the Pareto distribution. Quoting this last "natural law" on Tinder is strongly discouraged, btw.

It follows that, besides self-driving and new engine technologies, it's important to build the right infrastructure and fund the means of transport best suited to such short trips: e-bikes, kick scooters and electric scooters.

🏭 ff.1.4 Capturing CO2 will matter

Substack ↗

A very detailed Goldman Sachs report on decarbonisation by Michele Della Vigna discusses CO2 capture technologies. The goal? Making the technology economically accessible (at a cost below $100 / tonne of CO2).

Planting trees (the Natural sinks category in the chart) is a viable solution. But they're not enough: they could capture only 5% of the CO2 emitted each year.

Capturing CO2 where it's produced (Industrial CCUS) would seem to be the only solution, since the production processes for cement and steel, which will remain fundamental to our society, involve very polluting chemistry.

🇫🇮 ff.1.5 Bitcoin's energy consumption

Substack ↗

Given the incredible interest that has sprung up around Bitcoin recently, it seems only right to mention it too in this climate report.

It's estimated that the energy consumed to keep the blockchain running equals that of the whole of Finland.

There's a but: this figure is misleading if you don't account for the widespread use of renewable energy in mining and the ecological costs Bitcoin can prevent by transferring value digitally. We'll come back to this in a more detailed newsletter.

⚡ ff.1.6 Nuclear fusion

Substack ↗

In 1976 it was estimated that, given an investment of 9 billion a year, nuclear fusion would become reality by the early Nineties. Today, ITER (International Thermonuclear Experimental Reactor) is aiming for 2035 to demonstrate a "clean" nuclear fusion process.

In short, the road to nuclear fusion is very, very long. As described in the equally long article in The New Yorker.
