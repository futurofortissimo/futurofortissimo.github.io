🛠️ ff.123.1 The AI with a Swiss army knife

Substack ↗

When Tyler Cowen (theorist of the “great stagnation”, 🤔 ff.50.1 The oddness of the last 50 years) says he saw AGI (or a porno) on 16 April 2025, you have to stop and pay attention.

That day, OpenAI released the long-awaited o3.

In the past, ChatGPT was called out for trivial maths mistakes (⚠️ ff.48.4 ChatGPT's (mathematical) limits). The fix? Give it access to a calculator, for example.

o3 combines various “tools” in its thinking (chain of thought) - screenshots, web browsing, running Python code - reaching an IQ of 136 (top 1% of humans; last year the record was 94).

To give you an idea, it solves a maze and shows the path.

💎 ff.123.2 Minecraft beyond humans

Substack ↗

In all this, humans are increasingly the limiting factor. The bottleneck.

Just as AlphaGo (trained on human games) was beaten by AlphaZero (which trained “on its own”) ♟️ ff.18.4 Artistic chess: the king of chess.

AI understands more than we do, for example the language/structure of proteins (⚛️ ff.98.4 Quantum systems).

David Silver and Richard Sutton - pioneers of reinforcement learning (RL) - argue in a new paper that we've entered the Era of Experience: autonomous systems/agents that, in every field, explore the world and possible solutions without needing human confirmation.

Hunting for diamonds in Minecraft. Google/DeepMind's DreamerV3 is an example of this new technological phase.

Despite never having seen Minecraft played (and not knowing its rules), in 100 million steps it learned to break trees, make sticks and upgrade pickaxes. All the way to its first diamond block.

A voice outside the choir. Dan Hendrycks, director of the Center for AI Safety, argues instead that letting AI evolve independently will be our end.

🗺️ ff.123.3 The new GeoGuessr champion: o3

Substack ↗

To show off its ability to combine various tools, maps included, the ever-reliable Ethan Mollick had o3 play GeoGuessr.

I, instead, asked it when the sun will rise between two mountains I can see from my window.

It identified the mountains, used formulas I'd never heard of and even estimated a margin of error depending on where I stand to admire the sunrise (the churchyard or the cemetery car park).
