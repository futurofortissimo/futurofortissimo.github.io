🏎️ff.138.1 The fastest road

Substack ↗

Brachistochrone. Today I'll lose you with a single word.

It's the fastest path (but not the shortest!) between two points: the red curve, the most efficient at turning potential energy into kinetic energy.

The algorithms that increasingly regulate society do everything they can to optimise time. For example, my brother Luca pointed me to Openroute: travel times around a city, by various means of transport. At least the bike wins.

Then comes the race to optimise consumption: Google Maps and the least-polluting routes.

But in all this optimising, does anyone consider the human, psycho-physical side of things? Our mental health?

😊ff.138.2 The happiest road

Substack ↗

What if we asked instead which route maximises happiness?

According to Kathy Willis (Good Nature), you find it by lengthening your commute to include parks and greenery (or, better still, a riot of autumn colours).

“Forest bathing” is back with new details (💚 ff.51.1 Storytelling for green):

Natural horizons have a calming “fractal dimension” (the complexity of a skyline): our senses favour wide spaces with the odd tree here and there (like the savannah, where we come from in evolutionary terms).

Schools surrounded by greenery have beneficial effects on children's cognitive development.

PS. ChatGPT coined a term for the “brachistochrone of happiness”: euthymìchrone (εὔθυμος + χρόνος), the time of good spirits. εὔθυμος = “good-hearted, serene” + χρόνος = “time”.

🪵ff.138.3 Touch wood or iron?

Substack ↗

Oversights. Kathy Willis, however, laments the excessive, Platonic weight given to sight (against 1,500 papers on the psychological effects of “seeing nature”, only 40 ask whether it makes sense to “touch it”).

So how does nature affect us through the other senses?

Touching an oak, compared with marble and metal, has calming effects.

The same group - Japanese, naturally - also compared touching printed leaves with real ones (note the sock).

↗ pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov ↗ jphysiolanthropol.biomedcentral.com

🥌ff.138.4 Roomba or houseplants?

Substack ↗

It isn't just about perception: nature changes the microbiome (and so our physiological and psychological regulation, 🥽 ff.90.3 Microbes of stress):

Replacing gravel with forest floor in nurseries increases biodiversity in Swedish children in just 28 days, reducing biomarkers of inflammation and autoimmune disease. A single plant can repopulate our sterile homes with beneficial microbes (Roomba, I see you).

Bonus. Indoor spaces can be more polluted than outdoors, especially by volatile organic compounds (VOCs) released by plastic objects or detergents. A plant can reduce them.

🚬ff.138.5 I stay away from stress…

Substack ↗

Last but not least, breathing, another futuro fortissimo favourite (‍💨 ff.87.1 A short history of breathing).

Forest bathing is also relaxing thanks to α-pinene, a substance released by conifers (and found in cannabis).

And just three nights in a hotel that releases cypress fragrance are enough to boost signs of relaxation (measured with the “high-frequency” (HF) component of HRV, heart rate variability).
