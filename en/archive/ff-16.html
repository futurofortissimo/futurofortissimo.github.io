💰ff.16.1 Many trillions of dollars

Substack ↗

McKinsey estimates that to reach the COP26 climate goals we need to find 9 T$ (by T I mean trillions, a thousand billion dollars = 10^12 $) of investment a year, which is about a tenth of global GDP (100 T$) and 10 times the funding raised in this direction in 2021.

The "green premium" is the extra price to pay to offset the CO2 emissions produced. This contribution is the light-blue bar in the chart below. These costs are estimated for 2030 and add to the black bar, the baseline estimate for 2030 (there will be better efficiencies, so "base" costs will tend to be lower - today's price is shown by the dashed line).

A few things that jump out:

The green premiums of cement and steel are 50 and 25% respectively (producing these two materials "costs" us 10% of global emissions). Ethylene will cost practically the same in 2030 as it does today. The same will happen for paper, although in this case the green premium is smaller.

🌉ff.16.2 The cost of infrastructure

Substack ↗

Technological developments, but also infrastructure: another report by Goldman Sachs on COP26 tackles the cost of the infrastructure needed to keep warming to "only" 1.5 °C.

50 T$ from now to 2050 (up to 3 T$ in a single year). That's the bill for infrastructure alone: next-generation power plants, charging stations, adapting and maintaining infrastructure, and CO2 capture systems.

If we break investment down by type (adding up the contributions from now to 2050), we see that 30 T$ will go to new renewable energy facilities (8 T$ for solar, 13 T$ for onshore/offshore wind).

⚫ff.16.3 The (black) market for CO2

Substack ↗

To fund this spending, one idea is to tax pollution (and so make emissions paid for like physical waste, which is what happens with the TARI, Italy's waste tax).

Two fortissimo problems

The market is anything but "fair", with prices ranging from 7$/tonne (China) up to more than 150$/tonne (Sweden). The European area sits at about 75$/tonne. "Avoided" emissions. Right now companies can say "I'm polluting much less than I potentially could" and so receive CO2 emission credits that offset their emissions (traditional offsets). A bit like being allowed to eat a hamburger a day because I'm giving up a monthly trip to New York...

€50/tonne. McKinsey hopes for at least this level of tax on CO2 emissions to fund the costs of the transition.

In rich words:

A carbon pricing or emissions trading scheme could create incentives for individual stakeholders to reduce emissions. At a carbon price of €50 per tCO2e, an additional 21 percent of required capital, on top of the 40 percent already in the calculation, could be unlocked through 2050. A carbon price of €100 per tCO2e could unlock another 10 percent, giving more than 80 percent of all capital expenditures, including infrastructure, a standalone investment case. The remainder would require carbon prices of over €100 per tCO2e to create positive investment cases. How the European Union could achieve net-zero emissions at net-zero cost

The good news: in Europe we got to €80/tonne fairly quickly. Will the world follow?

🆕ff.16.4 Which startups to watch?

Substack ↗

Optimising technologies matters too, so let's take a closer look at some start-ups and at investment in fast-growing companies.

40 billion of the 700 estimated in 2021 was raised by start-ups or fast-growing companies. Growth was 20% every four months, a 3x increase compared to 2020.

The three main sectors (mobility, energy, and food and water) raised 90% of the funding; the most important companies are Northvolt, Rivian (mobility) and Helion, Commonwealth (nuclear fusion).

The other categories, including emissions capture, raised less money but saw a much bigger increase (10x) compared to 2020.
