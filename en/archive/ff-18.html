😲 ff.18.1 Why switching your brain off matters

Substack ↗

Awe helps us step out of the individual perspective that stays with us most of the time, lightening the weight we give to so many worries. That's the finding of a study in Human Brain Mapping.

3 types of video (funny, neutral, awe-inspiring) were shown to two groups. One group, however, had the task of counting the number of scene changes (so it was in a state of greater concentration).

Naturally, the "task-free" group got "distracted" more easily and started thinking about its own worries (activation of the default-mode network). But not while watching awe-inspiring videos (like nature videos with breathtaking views…)

Art can trigger a similar reaction, letting us "unplug" from the ego, from "myself".

L’arte non può essere compresa con l’intelletto, ma è percepita come un’emozione che presenta analogie con lafede religiosa o l’attrazione sessuale - un eco estetico. Bonnie Clearwater,West Coast Duchamp

🚽 ff.18.2 Artistic toilets

Substack ↗

Since we've mentioned Duchamp, what better way to bring him back than the ironic exhibition Best Seat in the House by the English collective Rankin?

1 person in 5 doesn't have a loo. Starting from this figure, and to raise awareness for the hygiene campaign promoted by WaterAid, the collective turned toilet seats into art. Elevating them to "the best seat in the house".

Toilets can make usfeel a little uncomfortable. Using the toilet seat as a canvas is an accessible and engaging way to put the spotlight on toilets and get people talking about them. Rankin

🖼️ ff.18.3 Art, between artificial intelligence and humans

Substack ↗

While we're talking about art, where does artificial intelligence fit into all this? Can computers be artists?

In The Artist in the Machine, Arthur I. Miller discusses the creative process, giving various examples of how computers can generate art independently or assist humans, creating a new synergy.

The creative process can be split into 4 phases, according to psychologist Graham Wallas (expanding on a model by Poincaré, The Art of Thought):

Conscious thought (the necessary substrate of information and education for fertile ideas) → Fertile soil to plant the seed in. Unconscious thought (freeing the mind, letting it wander, maybe watching the videos above) → Waiting for the seed to grow. Illumination (the "aha!" or "eureka" moment, seemingly out of nowhere) → The sprout shows its first leaf. Verification (backing up the illumination with a mental framework able to do so) → Growing and tending the sprout until it becomes a full plant

Digital green thumb? But can artificial intelligence go through all these processes? Can it make a leap "outside the box"?

♟️ ff.18.4 Artistic chess: the king of chess

Substack ↗

Sure, the computer is faster than humans and has a better memory, but in less "repetitive" processes, like creative ones, the machine seems inferior. As Garry Kasparov (beaten at chess by Deep Blue) put it: "I could smell a new kind of intelligence, a very strange kind of intelligence, inefficient and inflexible".

That was 1996.

Over the last 25 years, artificial neural networks have shown far greater flexibility: AlphaGo beat world champion Ke Jie by making a move that didn't mechanically follow common practice and millennia of game theory. A creative move.

"Deep, fruitful conversations between human and machine seemed like science-fiction stuff. For Go players, that moment has already arrived." Andy Okun (president of the American Go Association)

AI also figured out how to make better moves when not taught by humans, but by playing against itself and learning from scratch (see the difference between AlphaGo and AlphaZero). I'll leave you with the beautiful documentary that tells the story.
