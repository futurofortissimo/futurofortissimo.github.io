🚲 ff.20.1 The bicycle's revenge

Substack ↗

I don't know if you've noticed, but it seems the pandemic brought us more bicycles. At least in the Philippines. In fact, according to a World Bank survey, the bike was the only mode that saw its number of users grow.

21 metres each. Philippines aside, the good news is that something is moving in Italy too. Cycle lanes are growing steadily. Even if the data isn't up to date, you can see that on average in 2016 each person had their own 21.1 metres of cycle lane per capita (compared to 13 in 2008). For whatever the "metres-of-cycle-lane-per-capita" metric might mean.

Milan also recently approved a 240 million euro plan to boost connectivity in its hinterland (with 750 km of lanes, i.e. 0.07 metres per Lombardy resident?!). The goal: 20% of trips by bike by 2035. The challenge to Paris is on.

🇮🇩 ff.20.2 Jakarta's example

Substack ↗

You might think that urban redevelopment aimed at more sustainable mobility is only feasible on a small scale and in "blank slate" cities with little infrastructure tied to more traditional means of transport.

In 2015, Jakarta had about 3.5 million people. Now it has more than 10. Almost nobody had a car in 2015 and only 10% of the population got around by bike. Despite this, the city had the worst traffic in the world (Rome, damn it, only seventh).

The air wasn't exactly squeaky clean either.

And yet, thanks to a series of choices aimed at favouring public and sustainable transport, with a hand from COVID:

From 2019 to 2020 bike use increased 6 times (up to 10 in some areas). The Transjakarta public transport system reached 1M rides per day. In five years, access to public transport (stations less than 500m from home) doubled, reaching 92%.

One issue with getting around on two wheels is safety, which scares many people and takes away their freedom to choose the bike.

This fun map of San Francisco shows, as if they were ski slopes in Cortina, the difficulty level (= danger) of its streets.

Speaking of "safe streets", the startup Cowboy presented its bike-friendly maps, going beyond Google's, which often pay little attention to the needs of micromobility.
