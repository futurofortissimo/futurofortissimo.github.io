🪙ff.24.1 How the war accelerated crypto

Substack ↗

Cryptocurrencies, built on a decentralised network that no single sovereign power can control, were the easiest way to support the Ukrainian government in the first moments of the Russian invasion. Ukraine's minister of digital transformation proved very nimble on this, setting up a donation wallet in Bitcoin and Ethereum (the two most widespread cryptocurrencies).

💦ff.24.3 Turkey and Brazil defend their sovereignty

Substack ↗

In the book The Bitcoin Standard, Saifedean Ammous tells the history of the exchange of value, from barter to seashells; from gold to the dollar (today's global reserve). He argues that pegging currencies to something stable like gold or, nowadays, Bitcoin could lead to a more stable currency than leaving it to be terribly managed by a country's finance minister.

Why Bitcoin is a solution to inflation:

There will never be more than 21 million Bitcoin (the algorithm is programmed with this cap; about 19 million have been distributed so far). Nobody can control or increase their number, lowering their value, as happened instead with the dollar (and other "fiat" currencies) during the COVID crisis, which then caused the well-known price inflation (27% of the dollars on this planet were created in the last two years)

Bitcoin beats the Ruble. Speaking of war, Bitcoin and Putin: the value of the Bitcoin network overtook that of the Russian currency, after the fallout of the war.

Some currencies have lost value more than others in recent years. Especially in pandemic times. Take the Turkish lira, which in ten years went from being worth 0.45 to 0.063 € (!). Ark estimates that if 5% of the money in circulation were "captured" by Bitcoin, the value of the digital currency would rise to 3.5 times current values.

💦ff.24.4 Mining with gas

Substack ↗

Exxon Mobil announced it will use the energy produced from extracted (but surplus) gas to mine bitcoin. This gas is already being burned anyway, because it can't be fed into distribution channels. If it ended up in the atmosphere, it would pollute even more than in its "burned" form.
