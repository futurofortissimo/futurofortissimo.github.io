🌵 ff.37.1 Will it end like Westworld?

Substack ↗

There's a magnificent TV series: Westworld. Written by the Nolan brothers, with Hopkins and Aaron Paul (Pinkman from Breaking Bad, to be clear), it's about a Wild West theme park populated by unfeeling automatons, where you can unleash your most visceral impulses: sex, drugs, war. Over the first season, though, the humanoids seem to show clear signs of consciousness. I won't spoil anything else.

This series is just one of many narratives imagining the coexistence with robots that might await us, proof of how much this aspect of the future fascinates and frightens us. A future that is, after the latest Tesla AI Day, in my view closer than it seems.

🤖 ff.37.2 Tesla AI Day

Substack ↗

By now I think you all know Tesla doesn't want to stop at self-driving cars and robo-taxis. At Tesla AI Day 2022 (by now my favourite tech event) Musk presented major developments on Optimus, perhaps the most advanced humanoid robot in development.

Unlike Boston Dynamics' robots, Tesla wants them to move without coding every single movement. Musk wants to be able to give general instructions and leave the details to artificial intelligence. 73kg in weight, a 2.3kWh battery (roughly the energy of 2000 kcal), actuators to mimic fingers, knees and shoulders. The cost: $20k, in line with robot price projections for the coming years (though “humanoid” ones are more expensive, around $150k according to Christian Hubiki)

I'm torn on Musk's approach. A humanoid robot can be advantageous:

Better integration with the world around us; Safer human-machine interactions

At the same time, though, I have some doubts:

Trying to mimic every joint can be hard to develop and costly in energy; Wouldn't a robot too similar to us scare people, slowing its adoption?

Tesla has on its side great efficiency in scaling production and the know-how built up from electric cars (image processing, optimisation search algorithms, …)

🎮 ff.37.3 Haven't you tried NVIDIA?

Substack ↗

Sorry for the terrible pun above (in Italian, “Non ci hai NVIDIA?” sounds like “Aren't you jealous?”).

NVIDIA also recently held its annual conference, another event worth following, and not just for fans of Battlefield and GPUs ever better at simulating reality.

Different twins. To develop next-generation robots, you need to study their movements in detail, ideally in a fully virtual environment (digital twin). So simulation helps robotics, and the experience built up on Battlefield can come in handy.

On the hardware side, NVIDIA presented chips like the Jetson Orin Nano, which can be a common interface for all devices. It's a sort of Lego for artificial intelligence, a generic chip able to handle navigation and the smart management of sensor inputs.

Robotic metaverse. The intersection of these trends, where the line between digital and real blurs more and more, is obviously the metaverse. NVIDIA, with its Omniverse and the suite of tools/partnerships it has built over the years, is well positioned to seize the “meta” opportunity.
