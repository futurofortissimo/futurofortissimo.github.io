🏃🏻 ff.68.1 Ever faster

Substack ↗

Since we have less and less time, everything has to go faster. Including, above all, WhatsApp voice notes and YouTube podcasts.

For music classics, the Spotify playlist sped up songs (optimised for TikTok's 15 seconds).

Forrester: in three years (the geological era of 2006-2009) the acceptable loading time for a web page dropped from 4 to 2 seconds.

Patience, please. In short, we no longer know how to wait. To be bored. To sit still. Hence the cue to talk about anxiety again, picking up where we left off, 🎼 ff.44 Such anxiety! (pt. 1).

📏 ff.68.2 Ever more measured and controlled

Substack ↗

We measure everything, and I'm the first to do it. Sleep. Calories. Blood sugar. HRV (what? heart rate variability). Step count. Instagram followers. ff subscribers.

At the same time, more and more renunciations. More and more noes. Alcohol. Tobacco. Drugs. Added sugar. Aspartame. Processed food. Phone after 10pm. Coffee after 5pm. Food within 3 hours of bedtime.

Io ci provo a capirti e non capisco un cazzo (No, no, no)Pinguini Tattici Nucleari,Nonono

And yet, the more we measure, the more we set absolute limits, the more we hurt ourselves

mentally (without constant growth, a sense of failure and dropping good habits), physically (a slightly nerdy paper on the harmful effect of absolute thresholds in medicine when assigning treatments).

The dissenting voice. Bryan Johnson (💊 ff.49.4 The superman who takes 100 pills a day) counters with a less ego-centric view: we're simply evolving, as a society and as individuals augmented by AI and algorithms, and we need to accept that a little too.

In short, we're at a crossroads: follow optimisation and become Super Sapiens (🎼 ff.35 I'm fine, I'm a Super Sapiens) or settle for dark hair and a little belly.

☝️ ff.68.3 There's no limit to better (or to worse?)

Substack ↗

Seasoning the recipe of modern anxiety: the endless raising of the bar.

I ran my first marathon in 2 hours 45 minutes (3:54 min/km). In sporting terms I could easily feel I'd “made it”, since only 2-3% of marathon finishers, an already small subpopulation, manage to break 3h.

But once I shared the feat on social media, a friend's reaction was: “so what's next? 2 hours 30?”.

The message on repeat: constant growth and infinite limits (ever more far-fetched, partly because of social media and the algorithms that run it). Everyone's ego crushed.

🎁 ff.68.4 Less ego and control, more flow and presence: Naval's cure

Substack ↗

Before the summer I left you with a 2017 (!) podcast with Naval Ravikant (here's the transcript).

It's in this pleasant chat that we find a reflection to start from in looking for a cure for modern anxiety.

Non voglio che il mio senso del sé continui a svilupparsi e diventi più forte man mano che invecchio. Voglio che sia più debole e più smorzato in modo da poter vivere molto di più nella realtà di ogni giorno. Accettare la natura e i suoi limiti. Apprezzare il momento presente, come si fa da bambini. Naval Ravikant

Brad Stulberg, ex-McKinsey optimiser and author of Peak Performance, now proposes the concept of groundedness. Which is really Verga's oyster ideal adapted to our hyper-distracted, anxious days.

Here are the suggested exercises:

Accept your current state, remind yourself you're doing your best to be your best. Once you've acknowledged how things are, anchor yourself to them, committing to focus your attention on the present. Help yourself by seeking movement, action, which often comes with flow; that way your mind won't wander into the past or plan the future (even a fortissimo one).

Science seems to back this message of anchoring to the present: activity in the posterior cortex, linked to anxiety, drops when we accept the situation we're in. As also seen in 💚 ff.51.1 Storytelling for green.
